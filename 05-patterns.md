# 05 The Universals: Patterns Across Every Human Society

If chapter 04 mapped the drives that motivate individual humans, this chapter maps the patterns that show up when humans live together. The traits, institutions, and dynamics that have appeared in every human society anthropologists have ever studied.

The point of this chapter is to give you a fingerprint of humanity. If a feature shows up in pre-agricultural hunter-gatherers, ancient Sumerian cities, medieval China, modern Manhattan, and an isolated Amazonian village, it is part of human nature, not a cultural accident. Conversely, if a feature seems universal but turns out to be specific to a certain kind of society, it is information about your society, not about humans.

---

## 5.1 The anthropologist's checklist: Donald Brown's human universals

In 1991, anthropologist Donald Brown published a list of human universals. Behaviors, beliefs, and practices found in every culture that has been carefully studied. Several hundred items.

The full list is too long to reproduce, but the categories give the flavor:

**Language and cognition.** Grammar with verbs and nouns. Past, present, and future tenses or their functional equivalents. Number words in almost every language (the Pirahã are the documented exception). Logical concepts like "and," "not," "true," "false," and "same." Translation possible (any human language can express any concept, even if some concepts require many words).

**Music and dance.** Songs sung in groups. Rhythmic music. Dance. Lullabies. Music tied to ritual.

**Family and kinship.** Marriage in some form. Incest taboos, almost always including parent-child and sibling. Pair-bonding. Distinct roles for mother, father, and child. Extended kin networks recognized.

**Sex and reproduction.** Sexual jealousy. Modesty about some sexual or excretory behaviors. Concepts of beauty. Pubertal customs. Distinction between sex and gender.

**Politics and law.** Leadership, in some form. Conflict resolution mechanisms. Property concepts. Inheritance. Rules of conduct. Punishment for rule violations. Some form of authority.

**Religion and ritual.** Belief in supernatural agents (gods, spirits, ancestors). Rituals around birth, death, and major life transitions. Magical thinking in some domains. Funeral practices. Concepts of soul or spirit.

**Morality.** Sense of fairness. Reciprocity expectations. Moral judgment with positive and negative emotional content. Distinction between in-group and out-group, with different moral standards applying. Empathy.

**Aesthetics.** Decoration of the body. Adornment. Visual art in some form. Concepts of beauty. Color terms in almost every language (Everett reports none for the Pirahã).

**Tools and technology.** Tool use. Tool-making. Containers. Cooking. Fire control. Weapons. Clothing.

**Economic life.** Exchange. Specialization of labor by sex and age. Reciprocal gift-giving. Concepts of trade. Property.

**Knowledge and worldview.** Folk biology (classification of plants and animals). Folk physics. Folk psychology (attributing mental states to others). Beliefs about death. Beliefs about disease causation. Distinction between waking and dreaming.

**Emotions and self.** Crying. Laughing. Smiling. Anger, fear, sadness, joy, disgust, surprise. A sense of self. Self-consciousness. Inner subjective experience.

**Social structure.** In-group and out-group distinction. Status hierarchies. Sanctions for violations. Conflict over resources, including women and rank. Ethnocentrism. Distinct gender roles, even where similar tasks are shared.

This is what humans bring to any society, regardless of where or when. The cultural details vary enormously. The underlying scaffolding does not.

## 5.2 What this means

The universal list has implications that often surprise people.

**Religion is universal.** No society has ever been found without supernatural beliefs of some kind. Modern atheist communities are unusual and recent. This does not mean religion is true. It does mean that something in human psychology produces religion reliably.

**Hierarchy is universal.** No society has ever been found without status differences. Egalitarian hunter-gatherer bands hold dominance in check by acting together against would-be bosses (Boehm), while still recognizing differences in age and ability. Attempts to design fully equal groups have repeatedly produced new, informal hierarchies.

**Gender roles are universal in some form.** What men and women do in any specific society varies. The fact that there are distinct categories with distinct expectations does not. The strict 20th-century claim that gender is entirely socially constructed is not supported by the cross-cultural record. The opposite claim, that gender roles are fixed by biology, is also not supported. Both biology and culture are doing work, and both are real.

**Property is universal.** No human society has been found without some concept of ownership, although what can be owned and how varies dramatically.

**Conflict and violence are universal.** Every society studied has had lethal violence in some form. Whether organized warfare is universal among hunter-gatherers is disputed: Fry and Söderberg found most lethal events in 21 forager bands were personal killings, while Gat argues warfare is as old as our species. Pinker's argument in The Better Angels of Our Nature is that the rate has declined dramatically over the long arc, especially with the spread of states (some statisticians dispute that the data show a trend). It has not gone to zero anywhere.

**Art and decoration are universal.** Aesthetic experience and bodily adornment appear everywhere. The drive to make and respond to beauty is not optional.

**Storytelling is universal.** Every culture has narratives, myths, legends, fables. The narrative shape of human cognition is so deep that we struggle to think in any other mode for long.

## 5.3 The Big Five personality dimensions

Within the universal scaffolding, humans vary. The most empirically robust framework for that variation is the Big Five (sometimes called OCEAN):

| Trait | High end looks like | Low end looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Openness | Curious, imaginative, drawn to novelty and abstraction, artistic interests | Practical, conventional, prefers familiar, less interested in abstractions |
| Conscientiousness | Disciplined, organized, reliable, future-oriented, hardworking | Flexible, spontaneous, less focused on planning or schedules |
| Extraversion | Energized by social contact, talkative, assertive, seeks stimulation | Reserved, prefers solitude, lower social energy, sometimes shy |
| Agreeableness | Cooperative, trusting, warm, conflict-averse | Competitive, skeptical, willing to be unpopular, direct |
| Neuroticism | Prone to negative emotion, anxiety, mood swings, threat sensitivity | Emotionally stable, less reactive, calmer under stress |

Key facts:

- These five dimensions replicate across most large-scale societies studied (50 cultures in one study) but did not replicate in a Bolivian forager-horticulturalist society, the Tsimane.
- Each is roughly 40 to 60 percent heritable. The rest is some combination of environment and noise.
- Personality becomes more stable with age but keeps changing: consistency rises from about .54 in the college years to .64 at age 30 and about .74 between 50 and 70, and average conscientiousness and emotional stability rise between 20 and 40. Wholesale transformation is rare.
- The traits predict real life outcomes: conscientiousness predicts academic and professional success, extraversion predicts social network size, neuroticism predicts mental health risk, agreeableness predicts cooperative relationships, openness predicts creative production and political ideology.

A useful clarification: extraversion is not "social skill" and introversion is not "social anxiety." Extraverts gain energy from social contact; introverts spend energy and need to recover. Both can be skilled or unskilled. Both can be confident or anxious. They are different defaults, not levels of ability.

For practical purposes, treating the Big Five as a description of your temperament (not a fixed identity but a stable starting place) is useful. People who fight their temperament tend to burn out. People who work with it tend to compound.

## 5.4 Recurring patterns in civilizations

When you look at human societies at the macro level across centuries, you see patterns that repeat.

### The rise-and-fall cycle

Ibn Khaldun, in the 14th century, articulated a cycle: a dynasty rises in the desert or frontier with strong asabiyyah (social cohesion, group feeling), conquers the urban civilization, settles in luxury, gradually loses asabiyyah over generations, and is overthrown by a new vigorous group from outside. The cycle takes about three generations of 40 years each; by the fourth, the founders' prestige is gone.

Variants of this pattern have been observed elsewhere:

- Toynbee's "challenge and response" theory: civilizations rise when they face manageable challenges and respond creatively, decline when they lose the capacity to respond to new challenges.
- Peter Turchin's "secular cycles": political instability rises when elites overproduce and resources tighten, then resolves through some combination of war, reform, or collapse, in roughly multigenerational cycles.

The mechanism is plausibly: success produces wealth, wealth produces large privileged classes, privileged classes compete for finite positions, competition intensifies, cohesion erodes, an external or internal shock comes, the system cannot respond, the structure breaks down. Reformation may or may not follow.

This pattern is visible in Rome (rise of the republic, expansion, civil wars, late empire collapse), in dynastic China (Sui-Tang-Song-Yuan-Ming-Qing rhythm), in the Islamic caliphates, in many others. It is not deterministic. The pattern is real enough to bet on, not specific enough to time precisely.

### Inequality without active redistribution

In any society of any complexity, wealth and status concentrate. Pareto-type distributions describe income, business success, scientific output and athletic performance; one study of 633,263 researchers, entertainers, politicians and athletes found individual performance follows a power law, not a normal curve (O'Boyle and Aguinis 2012). A small fraction of participants produce or hold a large fraction of the total.

This is not a moral claim. It is a statistical observation. The mechanism is compound advantages: small initial differences accumulate over time. A slightly faster start produces a slightly better next opportunity, which compounds.

Societies that allow free competition without redistribution generate large inequalities, sometimes unstable ones. Societies that flatten inequality through forced redistribution generate other problems (underproduction, parallel hierarchies that capture the formal flattening). The recurring tension is the trade-off between productivity (which compounds with inequality) and cohesion (which requires limits on it).

### Religion and political authority

Across history, political authority and religious authority have been intertwined more often than separate. Theocracy is one extreme. Divine kingship is another. State-sponsored religion is a third. Active separation of religion and state, as in the post-Enlightenment West, is historically unusual and recent.

Even when explicit religion declines, secular substitutes appear. Nationalism, ideology, civic religion (rituals around the state itself), and political faith communities perform similar functions. The pattern is not the specific belief but the function: a shared meaning system that legitimizes authority and binds the polity.

### The cycle of moral panic and reform

Every society has a baseline level of moral disorder, sub-acceptable to some part of the population. Periodically, this gets framed as a crisis: a moral panic. Reformers mobilize, often religious or quasi-religious in their language. Some reforms succeed. Many fail. The cycle resets.

Examples: 19th-century temperance movements, 20th-century prohibition, periodic crackdowns on vice, the recurring waves of "the youth are degenerate" complaints (which people have made for millennia).

### Technological adoption curves

New technologies follow predictable S-curves. Early innovators take significant risks and produce dramatic outcomes (good and bad). Early adopters refine. Mass adoption follows. Late adopters lag. The curve takes anywhere from decades (electricity, automobiles) to a few years (smartphones, social media).

Each major technology produces winners and losers, reshapes social structure, and is later naturalized to the point that people forget what life was like before it.

## 5.5 Cooperation problems and how they get solved

A central feature of human social life is that cooperation requires solving certain problems that recur in every society.

**The free rider problem.** If the group benefits from cooperation but individuals can benefit more by free-riding (taking the benefit without contributing), cooperation collapses unless free-riding is punished. Every society develops mechanisms to detect and punish free-riding: gossip, ostracism, shame, formal punishment. Every population studied punishes unfair behavior to some degree, but how hard varies widely (Henrich et al. 2006, 15 populations).

**The trust problem.** Cooperation with strangers requires trust. Trust requires either past experience, reputation, or institutional guarantees. Small societies use direct experience and reputation. Large societies require institutions: contracts, courts, money, brands, ratings. The development of institutions to scale trust is one of the main themes of human history.

**The coordination problem.** Many situations require people to act in unison (agree on a currency, drive on the same side of the road, observe the same calendar). The solution is some shared convention, often arbitrary. Once established, the convention is hard to change. Path dependence is everywhere.

**The collective action problem.** Some goods require many people to contribute even though each individual contribution is small. Public goods (roads, defense, knowledge) suffer if too many contribute too little. Solutions include taxation (state-level), social pressure (community-level), and selective incentives (give contributors a private benefit).

**The asymmetric information problem.** Sellers know more than buyers. Insiders know more than outsiders. Solutions include reputation, signaling, trusted intermediaries, and regulation.

Every business and political institution is, in part, a solution to one or more of these problems. When the institution works, cooperation scales. When it fails, cooperation breaks down. The history of civilization is largely the history of building, breaking, and rebuilding these structures.

## 5.6 The asymmetries that produce inequality

A few specific asymmetries show up everywhere humans organize:

**The matter-energy asymmetry.** People who can mobilize more energy (in calories, in fossil fuels, in computation) can do more. Concentrations of energy access produce concentrations of capability.

**The information asymmetry.** People who know more or know first can position better. Information advantages compound.

**The capital asymmetry.** Stored wealth produces income. Income produces more stored wealth. This compounds across generations unless deliberately interrupted by taxation, inheritance limits, or upheaval.

**The network asymmetry.** People with more connections get more opportunities. Opportunities produce more connections. Networks compound.

**The position asymmetry.** People in central positions in any network capture disproportionate value, even if their underlying productivity is similar to others.

These asymmetries are not unique to humans. They show up in any system with feedback. But humans have unusual cultural and institutional capabilities to amplify or dampen them. Different societies make different choices about which asymmetries to enable and which to constrain.

The recurring observation is that **without active intervention, the asymmetries compound and inequality tends to grow, sometimes until it becomes politically destabilizing.** Most major societies have, at various times, imposed corrections (progressive taxation, anti-monopoly laws, inheritance limits, land reform, debt jubilees in the ancient Near East). Walter Scheidel's survey of history finds that peaceful reforms have rarely reversed inequality; large reductions have mostly followed mass-mobilization war, revolution, state collapse or plague.

## 5.7 Summary of the chapter

- Donald Brown's list of human universals shows hundreds of traits and practices that appear in every society. The universals form a cross-cultural fingerprint of human nature.
- Religion, hierarchy, gender roles, property, art, storytelling, and conflict are universal in form, varied in detail.
- Individual variation in personality is well captured by the Big Five (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism). Heritable, stable, and predictive of life outcomes.
- Civilizations follow recurring patterns: rise and fall cycles, drift toward inequality, intertwined political and religious authority, technological adoption curves, recurring moral panics.
- Cooperation requires solving recurring problems: free-riding, trust, coordination, collective action, information asymmetry. Institutions are the solutions humans have built across history.
- Asymmetries in energy, information, capital, network, and position compound naturally. Inequality grows without active intervention, until it becomes destabilizing.

## Applied: Business

Three powerful applications.

**First, design with universals, not novelty.** If your product asks customers to behave in ways that no human society has ever behaved (perfect rational choice, no status games, no in-group preference, no narrative), you are fighting human nature. If your product runs with the grain of universals (allows tribal identity, supplies status, tells a story, fits known cooperation patterns), you are pulling with it.

**Second, recognize the structural asymmetries in your market.** Energy, information, capital, network, position. Where you have an asymmetry, you compound. Where competitors have an asymmetry, they compound against you. The strategic question is which asymmetries you can build, defend, or borrow over time.

**Third, expect the rise-and-fall pattern in your category.** Most industries follow a life cycle: a burst of entrants, a shakeout, then a few large players. Those players often become complacent, and an outsider with more energy disrupts them. If you are in a mature category, the disruption is coming whether or not you can see it yet. If you are the disruptor, your job is to displace before you are displaced.

For Big Five and hiring: hire for fit with the role's actual demands, not for the personality you find pleasant. Sales roles tend to suit higher extraversion. Senior engineering roles tend to suit higher conscientiousness and lower agreeableness (so people will push back on bad decisions). Entrepreneurs score somewhat higher than managers on openness and lower on agreeableness, though the differences are small. Customer success tends to suit higher agreeableness. Trying to make any personality work in any role is expensive in time and burnout.

## Applied: Self

The universals provide a useful self-diagnostic. Are you running with them or against them?

- **Do you have a tribe?** If not, you are fighting a 300,000-year universal. Build one.
- **Do you have a meaning system?** Not necessarily a religion, but a coherent answer to "why am I here, what am I for?" If not, you will either drift into one (often poorly chosen) or pay a cost in psychological vulnerability.
- **Do you have ritual?** Not necessarily religious ritual. Repeated meaningful actions that mark transitions and times. Birthdays, family dinners, anniversaries, seasonal practices. Cultures abandon ritual at their peril. Individuals do the same.
- **Are you engaging with art and beauty?** Music, visual art, craft, performance. Cross-cultural human life involves regular contact with the aesthetic. Modern functional life often does not. Reinstate.
- **Have you accepted your temperament?** Big Five traits are stable. You can build skills on top of them. You cannot become a different temperament. Plan accordingly.
- **Are you naive about the rise-and-fall cycle in your industry or company?** Most stable employers are mid-cycle. Build for the next cycle before this one ends.
- **Are you aware of which asymmetries you are accumulating?** Energy (health, attention), information (skill, insight), capital (savings, equity), network (relationships), position (role, reputation). Each compounds. Each deserves deliberate attention.

The universals are not a checklist of obligations. They are a description of the conditions in which humans flourish. Living against them is possible. It is expensive. The cost shows up in mood, health, relationships, and effectiveness over time, and is often invisible until late.
