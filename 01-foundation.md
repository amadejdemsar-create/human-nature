# 01 Foundation: The Question and the Method

Before any content, we need to be precise about three things. What we are trying to understand, what "first principles" means here, and which common errors will trip you up if you do not see them coming.

---

## 1.1 What we are trying to understand

The question is deceptively simple: **what is a human?**

You can answer at many levels:

- A pile of cells (biology).
- A pattern of neurons firing (neuroscience).
- A negotiation between drives and inhibitions (psychology).
- A member of a tribe, family, and nation (sociology).
- A bearer of beliefs, narratives, and meaning (philosophy and religion).
- A node in a network of trade, language, and culture (anthropology, economics).
- A point in a 300,000-year story (history).

All of these are correct. None of them alone is sufficient. A good model of human nature has to make all of them compatible.

The goal is not a definition you could put on a flashcard. The goal is a working model rich enough to predict what humans will do in novel situations, why they suffer in the ways they suffer, why civilizations rise and fall in similar patterns, and why your own behavior often surprises you.

---

## 1.2 What "first principles" means here

A first-principles approach starts from the smallest possible set of foundational facts and reasons up. You build everything else from those facts.

For a physical problem, first principles would be conservation of energy, Newton's laws, or the laws of thermodynamics. For human nature, the first principles are:

1. **Humans are biological organisms.** Everything else, every culture, every philosophy, every economic system, is built on a body and brain produced by evolution. The biology is the floor. Nothing about humans can violate it.

2. **Evolution does not optimize for what we want.** It optimizes for reproductive success in past environments. Our preferences, instincts, and intuitions feel like they aim at our wellbeing. They aim at our ancestors' fertility. These are often the same. They are not always the same.

3. **Behavior is shaped by an interaction between evolved drives and cultural programming.** Neither is sufficient on its own. The drives are universal across the species. The cultural programs differ wildly. Both are real, and you need both to explain anything.

4. **Much of what we do is unconscious.** Much of it happens without conscious deliberation. In moral judgment especially, the reasoning usually comes after the verdict, not before it. This is well established in cognitive psychology and it is one of the most important facts about us.

5. **Humans are obligate social animals.** We did not evolve as individuals who happened to live in groups. We evolved as members of groups. A human alone is a human at risk: people without close, stable relationships show worse health and well-being. Almost every drive in our nature is calibrated against the presence of other humans.

6. **Cooperation at scale runs on shared fictions and learned norms.** Most species cooperate only with relatives or known reciprocal partners. Humans cooperate with strangers on a scale no other species matches. Shared stories, money and institutions play a large part, and so do learned norms (Harari stresses the first, Henrich the second). Money, gods, nations, laws, brands. These are not lies. They are tools.

That is the foundation. Everything else in this document is derived from those six claims plus the historical record.

## 1.3 Common errors about human nature

When you start exploring this topic, you will encounter five common mistakes. Avoid them.

### Error 1: The blank slate

The claim that humans are born as blank slates and that all behavior comes from culture and environment. This was a dominant view in 20th-century social science. It is largely false.

The reality: we are born with a rich set of evolved predispositions. Babies are not neutral. They look for faces, prefer their mother's voice, spot snakes and spiders faster than harmless objects (a head start for learning fear), develop language in a predictable order, and acquire moral intuitions without explicit teaching. Culture builds on top of this scaffolding, sometimes dramatically. It does not write on an empty page.

Why this matters: if you assume the blank slate, you will be confused that some patterns repeat across every culture. You will also keep being surprised by your own children.

### Error 2: Biological determinism

The opposite mistake. The claim that biology fully determines behavior and culture is just decoration. Also false.

The reality: biology sets ranges, defaults, and predispositions. Within those ranges, culture has enormous power. Two genetically similar people raised in 17th-century Tokyo and 21st-century New York will not behave the same way. The drives are stable. The expressions are plastic.

Why this matters: if you assume biological determinism, you will dismiss cultural change as superficial. You will miss the fact that human violence has fallen over the long arc of history (Pinker documents the trend, and some statisticians dispute it), that gender roles have shifted dramatically in a few generations, and that institutions can reshape behavior at scale.

### Error 3: The noble savage

The idea that pre-civilized humans lived in peaceful harmony with each other and with nature, and that civilization corrupted us. Popularized by Rousseau, reinforced by some 20th-century anthropology, and persistent in pop culture.

The reality: hunter-gatherer life had real strengths (egalitarianism within bands, healthy nutrition, strong social bonds, varied work). It also had real violence (scholars disagree on how much was organized warfare and how much personal killing, but the evidence shows both), high child mortality, no medicine, no protection from accidents, and no buffer against famine. It was not Eden.

Why this matters: any romanticism about the past makes it harder to see clearly. The past was different. It was not better in some uniform sense.

### Error 4: Modern people are different

The mirror image. The idea that we are categorically different from people 200 or 2,000 or 20,000 years ago because we have iPhones, modern medicine, or science.

The reality: human biology has kept changing: selection sped up over the last 40,000 years (Hawks et al. 2007), with lactose tolerance, altitude adaptations and disease resistance the best-known cases, while our basic drives and capacities stayed recognizable. Our basic drives, emotions and social needs are recognizably the same as those of a Bronze Age farmer or a Paleolithic hunter. Reasoning styles, moral priorities and even some perception differ across populations, so do not assume your own is the default. Their motivations, their tribalism, their status games, their need for meaning, their love and grief are ours. The proof is that you can read Homer or the Hebrew Bible or Sumerian poetry and recognize yourself.

Why this matters: if you think we are different, you will dismiss the evidence of history. History is mostly a record of humans being humans. The lessons are not optional.

### Error 5: One driver explains everything

The temptation to reduce all behavior to one master variable: sex, money, status, fear of death, power, the unconscious, class struggle, individual rational choice. Every major theorist who tried this is partly right and largely wrong.

The reality: human behavior is overdetermined. Multiple drives are active simultaneously. Status, sex, food, comfort, belonging, meaning, understanding, autonomy. They overlap, conflict, and substitute. A theory that reduces everything to one will explain a slice of human behavior and miss the rest.

Why this matters: when you read books and listen to thinkers, watch for the one-variable trap. The thinker who insists everything is really about X is usually missing the half of the picture that is not about X.

## 1.5 What this document is not

A few honest disclaimers.

- It is not academic. It is a synthesis aimed at practitioners. The sources are real and the claims are defensible, but this is a working model for thinking, not a dissertation.
- It is not neutral. It takes positions where the evidence supports a position. Where the field is genuinely split, it says so.
- It is not complete. Human nature is the largest topic there is. You could spend a lifetime on it. The goal here is to compress the most useful 5 percent into something you can read in an afternoon.
- It is not the last word. Treat it as scaffolding. Add your own reading, your own observations, your own counter-examples. The model improves with use.

## Applied: Business

The single most important business application of this chapter is recognizing the **blank slate fallacy in marketing and product design**. Most marketing copy assumes humans are rational actors who weigh features and pick the best option. They are not. They are biological organisms running ancient software that responds to status signals, in-group cues, fear, belonging, identity, and stories. If your positioning treats your buyer as a CPU comparing specs, you will lose to a competitor whose positioning treats the buyer as a status-anxious tribal storytelling primate. Most great brands are doing the second whether they admit it or not.

The second application is the **one-variable trap in strategy**. Founders often anchor on a single explanation for why their business will work or why a competitor is winning ("they have better tech," "we have better product-market fit," "their marketing is better"). Real business outcomes are overdetermined. Multiple drivers are usually active. Train yourself to ask "what else explains this?" before locking in a theory.

## Applied: Self

The most useful personal takeaway from this chapter is the **reasoning-often-follows-the-verdict** point. When you find yourself confidently explaining why you made a decision, hold lightly. For moral and gut-level choices especially, the reasons often arrive after the verdict, and the real causal factors are often invisible to you. This is not a defect. It is how the system works. The practical move is to notice when an explanation feels suspiciously clean ("I left that job because of the commute") and ask what unconscious drivers might also be in play (status, fear, boredom, conflict avoidance, a sense that you were falling behind a peer). The point is not to distrust yourself. The point is to recognize that self-knowledge is a project, not a starting condition.

A second takeaway: be careful with the noble savage and modern-people-are-different errors when you reason about your own situation. You are not uniquely cursed by the modern world, and there is no past life in which you would have been free of the problems you now have. Different problems, same nervous system.
