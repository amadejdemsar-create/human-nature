# Human Nature From First Principles

A complete, beginner-friendly, first-principles guide to what humans are, why we are the way we are, and what has actually driven the species across the ~300,000 years we have existed.

The treatment is encyclopedic on purpose. The goal is to build one durable mental model that explains psychology, history, business, politics, religion, technology, and your own behavior using the same underlying primitives.

Every chapter ends with two applied-lens blocks: how the chapter's ideas show up in business and GTM, and how they show up in self-understanding and personal life. The applied blocks are short. The core content is long.

## The thirty-second version

If you only have one minute, here is the entire document compressed.

Humans are biological organisms shaped by roughly 3.5 billion years of evolution. The body and brain you carry now were shaped mostly by hunter-gatherer life in small bands in Africa over some 300,000 years, and selection has kept working since. Evolution does not optimize for happiness or truth. It optimizes for reproductive success in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness.

Between roughly 70,000 and 30,000 years ago, by Harari's dating, humans began to share stories about things that do not exist. Many archaeologists see a gradual build-up that began earlier. That unlocked cooperation in groups far beyond the people one mind can know personally (Dunbar put that at about 150, a rough estimate that later statistical work does not support firmly). Money, gods, nations, corporations, brands, ideologies. All of these are shared fictions. They are also the operating system of civilization. No other species is known to build anything like them.

For tens of thousands of years humans lived mostly in small bands and were relatively egalitarian within them, though not every pre-farming society fit that pattern. Then, around 10,000 years ago, agriculture produced food surplus. Surplus made hierarchy, property, taxes, kings, armies, slavery, and writing possible. Wealth gaps grew with domestication, though early farming did not always produce rulers. Our basic biology changed little, though selection kept working. The environment changed fast. We have been living in an environment our genes were not built for ever since.

Around 2,500 years ago, the Axial Age produced the first formal moral philosophies and universal religions. Around 500 years ago, the Scientific Revolution discovered that observation and math could overrule authority. Around 250 years ago, the Industrial Revolution unleashed energy density that fundamentally changed material life. Now we live in conditions of unprecedented abundance, with brains tuned for scarcity, status competition, and tribal cohesion in small groups.

What drives humans, across every culture and every century, is a stable set of needs: survival, reproduction, status, belonging, meaning, understanding, autonomy, comfort. The cultural surface varies. The drives do not. Status is one of the strongest and least admitted drivers of adult human behavior.

The clash between our evolved nature and our current environment explains part of modern dysfunction, such as what we eat and how little we move. It does not explain all of it. Civilization is a technology for cooperating at scale despite our nature. It is real, it is fragile, and it is not guaranteed to continue.

That paragraph is true at every scale: an individual life, a company, an empire. Everything else in this document is detail, evidence, and application.

## When this document is wrong

A first-principles model is a compression. It will be wrong at the edges. Specifically:

- It will overstate stability of human nature where culture is doing more work than the document credits.
- It will understate cultural variation because the goal is to find what is shared.
- Some claims (the agricultural revolution as a net loss for human welfare, the exact mechanism of the cognitive revolution, the universality of certain behaviors) remain contested. The main counter-positions are listed in the sources at the end of each chapter.
- Specific dates for prehistoric events have meaningful ranges of uncertainty. When this document says "10,000 years ago," read it as "give or take a few thousand years."

The model is good enough to be useful. It is not good enough to be final. Use it as scaffolding, not gospel.

## Applied lens: why bother

Two reasons.

**For business and GTM**, every product is bought by a human, every sale is closed by a human, every team is run by a human. If you do not understand what humans actually want under the surface (status, belonging, identity, relief from anxiety, a story they can tell about themselves), you will keep building products people compliment but do not buy. The strongest marketers, founders, and operators run on a model of human nature whether they articulate it or not. Making the model explicit is leverage.

**For self-understanding**, most of what drives you is unconscious. Your stated reasons for choices are usually constructed afterward. Your status drive is probably stronger than you let yourself admit. Your tribal loyalties shape your beliefs more than your beliefs shape your loyalties. Without a clear model of what your evolved nature actually wants, you will keep being surprised by yourself, including in ways that derail your goals.

The rest of this document is the model.
